Are cheap Magic cards more collectible than popular ones

Market analysis Card prices TCG business

Frank's Hobbies has a new video with a title that sounds like a contradiction. Cheap cards are the most collectible. His point is that popularity and collectibility are not the same thing, and he is mostly right.

Popularity is mass demand, everyone chasing the same card. Collectibility is personal. Frank collects what he likes, nice art, a favorite character, a color he likes, cards under 5 dollars for a binder. A card nobody is hyping is easier to collect, because nobody is competing with you for it.

Then he makes the market claim, and this is where it gets interesting. Cheap cards get bought quietly by people who never sell them back. Each copy that lands in a binder is one fewer copy on the market. Sellers run out at one price, then the next seller is higher with ten copies, then the next higher still with six. He calls these walls of resistance.

His best example is a Pokemon card, Team Aqua's Kyogre from Double Crisis. He watched it climb from 25 dollars to 100 and wishes he had bought in. He says it is around 300 dollars now. He is underselling his own example. TCGplayer market is about 705 dollars as of November 16, up 9.3 percent in the last month alone.

Here is the part to be careful about. Kyogre had a real collector base, a small 2015 set, and an iconic Pokemon. Most cheap cards do not have that. A 25 cent card with no art and no one looking for it stays a 25 cent card, and a binder full of them is still bulk. Frank admits he cannot predict which cheap cards will rise.

The takeaway is narrower than the title. Do not buy popular cards just because they are popular, and do not buy cheap cards just because they are cheap. Buy the cheap cards people will still want in five years, strong art, a beloved character, an original printing. Those are the ones where the wall of resistance is real. The rest is bulk with a story attached.

Sources: Frank's Hobbies on YouTube and TickerMint on Team Aqua's Kyogre-EX.
