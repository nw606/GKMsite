How should you price cheap Magic cards so multi-card orders actually make money

Selling cards Card prices TCGplayer

In his latest video, Sam at Leumas Card Craze shows how he prices about 150,000 trading cards without researching each one by hand. His old way took a minute or two per card, and at that scale it just never ends. So he uses automated price bands and floors instead, with TCGplayer market price as the starting point, and he prices by platform because fees and shipping change the answer.

The number that stuck with me is the gap between a one-card order and a bigger one. On eBay, he says a single card sold at his $1.59 floor leaves about $0.11 after fees, shipping, and supplies. Add a second card and the same order makes about $0.70 per card. That is why his system is built around getting buyers to add more cards. Cards at $2.99 and under carry buy 2 get 1 free and buy 5 get 3 free deals on his eBay store.

The same idea shows up on TCGplayer. Cards with a market price between $0.15 and $0.19 get listed at $0.15, basically as a draw. Buyers come for quantity and then add 30 or 40 cards in higher bands. He points to a 109-card morning order that still made about $10 in profit after shipping because the rest of the cart carried the order.

There is a catch, and he does not hide it. Cheap prices bring volume, and volume brings work. He talks about waking up to 330 cards to pull, rising to about 400 across platforms, and sometimes raising his floor just to slow orders down until he can catch up. So a floor is not only a profit line. It is also a workload valve.

If you sell Magic singles, stop judging a cheap card by itself and ask what order it is likely to land in. Set a floor that covers fees, shipping, and packing on a single-card sale, then give buyers a reason to add a second and third card. Be honest about your pulling time, because the cheapest profitable price on paper can still bury you in envelopes.
